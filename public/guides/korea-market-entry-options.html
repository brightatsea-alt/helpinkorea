Representative, distributor, branch or subsidiary? Choosing your Korea market-entry model

Updated September 2026 · 8 min read

Korea is a market where relationships and paperwork both matter, and where a foreign company that shows up “with nobody on the ground” tends to be politely ignored. There are four common ways to fix that. Each trades money for control.

1. A local representative

You appoint a Korea-based person or firm as your named representative. They carry your business card, attend meetings, chase quotes, vet partners, sit in on negotiations and report back weekly. Legally you remain a foreign company; commercially, Korean counterparts have a Korean-speaking human to call.

Why SMEs start here: it costs less than one junior hire, can be switched off with 30 days' notice, and produces something the other models don't — a written, first-hand picture of the market before you commit capital. The main risk is choosing a generalist; pick someone who already knows your industry's buyers.

2. An exclusive distributor

The classic route for physical goods. The distributor imports, stocks, sells and services; you get orders and a margin cut. It is fast and cheap to start, but you lose sight of end customers, and “exclusive” agreements in Korea are notoriously hard to exit if the distributor underperforms. Always define minimum purchase volumes, a term (2–3 years), and termination for non-performance, and register your trademark in Korea before signing — otherwise some distributors register it themselves.

3. A branch office

A branch is the foreign company itself operating in Korea. Registration is through a foreign-exchange bank and the court registry, and the branch can invoice, hire and pay tax locally. The catch: the parent company is fully liable for everything the branch does, and branch profits are taxed in Korea with limited flexibility. It suits consulting or engineering firms with two or three Korean staff.

4. A subsidiary

A Korean limited company owned by the parent. This is the “real” entry: limited liability, full control, easy hiring, eligibility for tenders that require a Korean entity. It needs FDI notification under the Foreign Investment Promotion Act, paid-in capital (KRW 100 million is the practical minimum to qualify as FDI), a registered address, a resident director or a Korean representative director, and monthly bookkeeping. Budget 2–3 months and a local accountant from day one.

The path most foreign SMEs actually take

  1. Months 0–6: local representative on retainer. Map the buyers, meet the top ten, sign one or two pilot customers or a non-exclusive distributor.
  2. Months 6–18: once there is revenue, either commit to an exclusive distributor with performance clauses, or
  3. Month 18+: open a subsidiary, often hiring the representative as the first country manager — with the hand-over terms already written into the original retainer agreement.
One clause that saves a lot of pain: whichever model you choose, put a “transition” clause in the first contract — what happens to contacts, files, customers and commissions when you upgrade to the next model. Korea has a strong culture of personal relationships; you want those relationships transferred, not lost.

Things that are the same in every model

  • Trademark first. Korea is first-to-file. Filing costs a few hundred dollars; recovering a squatted mark costs years.
  • Data & product compliance. If you sell to consumers online you may need a PIPA domestic representative; cosmetics, devices, food, electrical goods and children's products each have a Korean registration or KC certification path.
  • Korean-language paper. Quotes, catalogues and the privacy policy in Korean are not optional if you want Korean procurement teams to say yes.
